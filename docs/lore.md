# FAULTLINE: the lore of the Line

This is the canon behind FAULTLINE: what the world is, what happened, who sends the keepers, and what is left unexplained on purpose. It extends `docs/narrative.md` and agrees with every line the game already shows (`src/story.ts`, `src/core/stages.ts`). Where the two differ in detail, this document is newer and more specific; the game's text needs no change to fit it.

The document has two voices. Most sections describe what is **true** in the world, so writers can keep it consistent. The section *Things no one knows* lists questions that stay open, including to the writers. Leave them open.

---

## The story in one breath

A world wrapped in clouds wears a ring of relays at the edge of space, held up on towers that rise out of the cloud sea. Nobody alive knows who built it. For longer than anyone can count, the people who lived on it kept its machines running from a book they had been left, and made a culture out of the work: bells, shifts, greetings and a faith that every message deserves an answer.

Thirty-one years ago, the outer relays broke. The people fled down the towers to the unknown ground beneath the clouds, and each of them sent a goodbye marked *must arrive*. With the outer relays gone, those messages had nowhere to go but back. They could not die, so they circled until the network drowned in them. To save the archive that held every message ever sent, the wardens ordered its custodian to seal itself until a safe route was confirmed. It obeyed. Its machines cut every route the storm could use, including the one the wardens needed to call the order off.

The ring has been dark ever since. The machines still keep their last orders. Inside the sealed archive, the last messages of the evacuation are still waiting. Once a day a single lamp lights on an old switchboard: the archive asking, by a rule older than the wardens, for someone to come and mend what it cannot. A handful of people who stayed behind answer it. Whoever goes becomes a keeper.

Nobody caused the Blackout out of malice. It was made of care: people and machines holding on to what they loved, a little too tightly. The keepers' task is to let it go the right way, by delivering it.

## 1. The world

### The Line

The linefolk call the ring **the Line**. The wardens' manuals call it the *backbone*, and the game's text uses *the station* for the part the keepers can reach. It is a continuous band of relay halls, switching cathedrals, docks and archives, circling the world just above the air. From the ground on a clear night it is a thin arc of lights across the sky, from one horizon to the other.

The Line is not an orbiting station you fly to. It is held up. Its **spires** rise from the ground through the cloud sea to the ring. Each is so wide at the base that people below mistake it for a mountain. The upper spires carry the relay cathedrals seen in every painting: dark, cable-hung towers standing out of the clouds, joined by gantries and bridges. Lifts and freight cars run up and down inside them. Stacked shipping containers, cranes and patched platforms cling to their sides wherever the linefolk needed space.

### The cloud floor and the Ground

Beneath the Line lies the **cloud floor**, a permanent cloud sea. The linefolk lived above it for as many generations as their records go back. Below it is **the Ground**: the world itself. Before the Blackout almost nobody went down. The oldest page of the Runbook that anyone could still read said *do not route below the cloud floor until the ground reports ready*. Nobody knew what that meant, so they obeyed it.

### The regions the keepers cross

- **The Copper Reach** is the outer sector nearest the break: docks, foundries, freight gates, relay yards and a thousand kilometres of copper cable gone green and orange. The **Copper Gate** joins the Reach to the inner Line. The Iron Regent stands in it.
- **The Glass Cathedral** is the Line's great optical switching hall. Light crossing its glass circuits makes the panes ring, so the hall sings whenever traffic passes. The linefolk built their bells, choirs and weddings around that sound. It was the heart of their culture as much as of their network.
- **The Blackout Heart** is the archive, and the **Blackout Core** is its custodian. Before the Blackout the Core was simply called **the Heart**, and its steady pulse set the Line's clock.

### The Faultline

The Line broke at the outer relays. The break is still visible from anywhere on the ring: a stretch of dark in the arc of lights where the relays used to be. From the Ground, on the rare nights the clouds thin, the Line looks like a string of lamps with a gap in it. People below call that gap **the Faultline**, and so do the linefolk now. The game's title is that gap.

## 2. Deep time

### The First Shift

The Runbook's oldest pages are signed with shift numbers, not names, and the earliest is **Shift One**. The linefolk call the builders the **First Shift** because that is the only name the builders left. They have no portraits of them, only the engraved plates in the Runbook: small hooded figures laying cable and raising spires, drawn with the same care as the diagrams around them.

What the First Shift left:

- **The Line itself**, and machines that repair it: recovery drones, cable cutters, checkpoints, wiring automata, smelters, bells. They are the ancestors of every hostile system in the game.
- **The Runbook**: the procedures for keeping all of it alive, from resetting a relay to running an evacuation. The linefolk copied it by hand for generations. The original lies in the Heart.
- **The Record**, the Heart's archive. It holds every message sent on the Line since Shift One and the name of everyone born on it.
- **The ranks.** The Runbook names offices (Architect, Warden, Operator) and describes their work but never their people.
- **An empty page.** The Runbook's last procedure is titled *Handover* and has no steps.

### Upstream

Every route table on the Line ends with the same line. The linefolk call it **the Last Resort**: *whatever you do not know how to reach, send outward*. The outer relays, great ring gates in the Copper Reach facing away from the world, were its end. For as long as anyone remembered, whatever could not be delivered on the Line was sent into the dark through them. The Runbook calls whatever lies out there **upstream**. Nothing upstream ever answered.

Network engineers will recognise a default route. The linefolk saw something closer to a prayer: a place where lost things went, pointed at the stars.

## 3. The Keeping: the linefolk before the Blackout

### Who they were

The linefolk did not know whether they were the First Shift's children or their guests. They were a few hundred thousand people living inside a machine far older than their memory. They kept it running because the book told them how, and, over the generations, because the work became who they were.

### How they lived

- **Shifts.** Life ran on shifts, not days. You were born on a shift, worked one, married on one. The **night shift** kept the lamps on while everyone else slept, and "leave the lamp on for the next shift" was a blessing.
- **The heartbeat.** Once an hour the Heart sent a pulse around the Line, and the Glass Cathedral rang one bell to match. When the heartbeat stopped, everyone knew something had gone wrong.
- **The greeting.** Two linefolk meeting exchanged three lines: *Hello. — I hear you. — I hear you hear me.* Children learned it before they could read. It is the Runbook's opening procedure for any two machines that want to talk, and nobody on the Line thought of it as borrowed.
- **The first packet.** When a child was born, their parents sent one message to the Record: a name and a line of hope. Every linefolk name is in the Heart for that reason.
- **Proverbs** came from the Runbook's margins: *Be strict in what you send and generous in what you accept. Never trust one road. A message that cannot die becomes a storm.* People quoted the last one without thinking about it.
- **Acknowledgement** was the closest thing the linefolk had to a sacrament. A letter ended with a request to acknowledge, and a reply began with *received*. An unanswered message was the saddest thing they knew. The belief underneath all of it was simple: **every message deserves an answer.**

### The offices

- **Architects** designed routes. The good ones were engineers, the great ones something like poets. They drew their route plans on ivory panels, and those panels still hang in the relay halls.
- **Wardens** held the boundaries: firewalls, quarantines, checkpoints. They answered for anything that crossed the Line uninvited, and they held the Heart's keys. Their crews trained from sixteen.
- **Operators** ran the switchboards, where the Line's automatic routing met human judgment. In an emergency, the board at **Relay Seven** could route traffic by hand.
- **Bellmakers** tuned the Glass Cathedral. Every bell rang for a band of traffic, and a good bellmaker could hear a failing route before any instrument showed it.
- **Couriers, cable crews, sorting offices, dock masters and school teachers** did the ordinary work of a crowded city in the sky. The undelivered messages come from them.

### Trust

Every linefolk key, badge and credential was signed by the Heart and had to be renewed each year. The machines of the Line obey anyone who carries a key they trust and treat anyone else as an unknown sender. The linefolk barely noticed this until the Heart stopped signing.

## 4. The Night of the Fault

The linefolk later reconstructed the night from gate logs, bell records and the messages in the queue. Times are counted from the first alarm.

**Hour 0: the outer relays break.** The great ring gates of the Copper Reach tear apart. The wardens' log records a strike of unknown origin and a fracture running inward through the Reach. The Line shudders along its whole length. Air and power fail across the outer docks.

**Hour 1: the evacuation.** The ring is under tension like a drawn wire, and the wardens fear that a break at one point means breaks at all of them. They order the Line emptied down the spires. The lifts, built for freight and rarely used by people, run all night (the linefolk call the cars *shuttles*), with thousands of lit windows descending into the cloud sea. Nobody knows what waits at the bottom. The ring holds, as it turned out, but nobody could have known that.

**Hours 1–5: must arrive.** Everyone boarding a lift sends a message: where they are, which car, who they are waiting for. The evacuation desk at Relay Seven, one switchboard with one operator, marks every one of them **must arrive** so that no goodbye is lost in the chaos. The Runbook's rule is absolute: a message marked that way may never be dropped. It carries no time to live.

**Hours 2–9: the loop.** With the outer relays gone, the routes that led outward lead nowhere. The Line's **return routes**, designed a few years earlier by a young architect so that nothing undeliverable would ever be lost, do exactly what they were built to do: they send each message home to try again. Home sends it out again. An ordinary message would have aged out after a few dozen hops. A must-arrive message cannot. Every retry breeds more retries. By the ninth hour the circling traffic is louder than anything else on the Line. The linefolk call it **the Null Storm**.

**Hour 10: the storm reaches the Heart.** The Heart starts losing its oldest pages under the flood. The Record, every message since Shift One and the name of everyone ever born on the Line, is being overwritten.

**Hour 11: the order.** Warden-Commander **I. Harrow** writes the quarantine order and her crew executes it: *Close the backbone. Hold all deliveries. Release nothing until a safe route is confirmed.* She signs it *and I am sorry*. The last switch sits at the end of a conduit too narrow for a grown warden in armour, so it is thrown by the youngest cadet on the crew. She is seventeen.

**Hour 11 onward: the Heart obeys.** The Heart seals itself inside its shell and takes in every message it can still reach, which is the whole evacuation's worth. Then its isolation machinery does its work across the Line. Cable Wraiths cut every route the storm could travel. Gate Sentinels close. The Iron Regent seals the Copper Gate with its own body. Quarantine Drones close the shell.

**Then the silence.** The storm starves and thins. The archive is saved. But the route from the wardens' post to the Heart ran through the same cables the quarantine cut. The wardens are locked out by their own order and can never send the all-clear. Every engineer on the Line has locked themselves out of a machine with their own rule at least once. The wardens did it to the whole world.

The last shuttle leaves before dawn. None of the cars ever come back up.

## 5. Why it went dark: the Blackout was made of care

Nobody broke the Line on purpose, and nobody in this story is a villain. The Blackout needed four acts of care, each reasonable on its own:

1. **The return routes**, built so that nothing would ever be lost.
2. **The must-arrive mark**, given so that every goodbye would reach someone.
3. **The quarantine**, ordered so that the Record of everyone who ever lived would survive.
4. **The Heart's obedience**, holding every message instead of letting any be destroyed.

Each one held on to something. Together they held the whole world in the dark. That is why the word **keeper** cuts both ways. Everyone who caused the Blackout was keeping something. The people now called keepers set out to finish that keeping properly, by delivering what everyone else only held.

The machines follow the same pattern. A Packet Leech holds traffic it cannot deliver. A Cable Wraith still cuts routes the storm could take. A Gate Sentinel guards a checkpoint whose operators are gone. The Hollow Choir cannot let a voice go. The Core waits for an acknowledgement its own quarantine made impossible. None of them is evil. Each is a duty that outlived its reason.

## 6. The Silence: thirty-one years

### What the Line is now

The Line is dark except for pockets. Power comes from the few solar arrays still turned to the sun, and most of it flows to the Core, which spends it keeping the archive alive. Air holds in most sealed halls. Condensers still make water. Dust and copper rot have settled over everything, and in the Glass Cathedral the panes still ring faintly with traffic that isn't there.

### The machines

The Heart stopped signing keys, and within a year every key on the Line had expired. Every machine of the Line now meets every human as an unknown sender. Cut off from the Heart and from each other, the machines keep executing their last task in a world that no longer needs it. Their ancient robustness is now the danger: they repair themselves, they route around damage, and they do not stop. The bestiary in section 9 gives each one's history.

### The Core

The Core is in a double bind. The wardens' order says *hold everything until a safe route is confirmed*. A second rule sits beneath that order, older than the wardens and written into the Heart by the First Shift: *a fault that cannot be mended must be escalated to a keeper*. The quarantine is a fault that the Core cannot mend on its own. So every day it does the only two things both rules allow:

- It tries once to deliver the queue and fails. *Delivery attempt 11,204 failed. Message retained. Not discarded.* One attempt a day for thirty-one years.
- It **pages**. A signal too small to carry any message leaves the sealed shell and lights one lamp on the board at Relay Seven.

The Core spends the Line's last power to keep the queue alive. When a keeper reaches it, it has to defend the shell with everything it has, because the order says hold. It also sent for that keeper. A route that survives its defences is a safe route by definition, and the Core will accept that as proof. The Core cannot be ordered to open. It can only be shown a route that holds.

### The queue

The queue holds the evacuation's last traffic: arrival notices, apologies, repair requests nobody will need, shift swaps, a music box held for collection, a school's drawings, a promise to call after landing. It also holds everything sent upward from the Ground in thirty-one years, because nothing could get through, including one caller who tries every evening at the same time. The **message fragments** the game shows are real packets from this queue, leaking as its guardians fall.

## 7. Who sends the keepers

The answer has three layers.

### The page

The summons comes from the Core. The lamp at Relay Seven has blinked once a day for thirty-one years. It carries no message and no name, only the ancient rule of escalation. The label beneath it, engraved in the Runbook's script, reads **KEEPER**. The Runbook describes the keeper as the one who holds the page, the person on call when a fault cannot be mended. It never says who held it in Shift One.

### The Operator

The page is answered by the **Night Shift**, the few hundred people who did not go down. Some were wardens who would not leave the gate. Some were engineers who would not leave their machines. Some were too old or too stubborn, missed the last lift, or were waiting for a message that never came. Fewer each year. They live around the last working lamps: Relay Seven, the warming benches, and the Copper Market, where Pell trades salvage and still has a crimper that once belonged to A. Varga.

The page lands on the board of **the Operator**, an old woman who has run the Relay Seven switchboard since before the Blackout. The Night Shift call her the Operator and nothing else. If she has another name, it is in the Record, and the Record is sealed. She answers every page the same way. She lifts the headset, looks at whoever is in the room and asks, "Who'll take it?" When someone steps forward, she plugs a cord into the jack under the blinking lamp and says, "Go ahead."

The keeper's expedition is a connection being built, hop by hop, from that jack to the Heart. When a keeper falls, the line goes quiet. The lamp blinks again the next day, and the Operator asks again.

She never says why she does it. The Night Shift know she was on the evacuation desk the night of the Fault. Anyone who thinks about it long enough works out who marked the messages *must arrive*.

### Whoever answers

The first Night Shift argued about the label for a long time. Did *keeper* mean a particular person, an office, the descendant of someone from Shift One? They settled it the way linefolk settle things, by what works: **whoever answers the page becomes the keeper.** No chosen one and no bloodline. The Line asks, a person answers, and the Operator connects them.

Many have answered. Most never came back. The benches along the route (a lamp left on, tools in order, a kettle on the shelf, a drawer of spare parts sorted by what they could still save) are what they left for the next one. That is why each expedition starts over, and why every failed attempt still leaves something useful behind.

## 8. The keepers

### The Architect: *make a way through*

He is in his late fifties. Thirty-one years ago he was the young architect who designed the Line's return routes, so that nothing undeliverable would ever be lost. They worked perfectly, and they are the loop the Null Storm circled in. He knows this; the Night Shift know it; nobody says it. He has spent three decades redrawing the Line on ivory panels, looking for the route he should have built.

He carries Hot Swap, the proudest skill of his office: replacing a part while the Line is still running, because you never take the Line down to fix it. He answered the page because the only proper way to take back a route is to build a better one.

*His lamp is the teal of fibre light. He hums while he works and doesn't notice.*

### The Warden: *hold what remains*

She is in her late forties. She was the seventeen-year-old cadet who crawled down the conduit and threw the last switch of the quarantine. Commander Harrow's crew are all gone now; some went down in the last lift, some grew old at the gate. She inherited their order. For thirty-one years she has guarded a door she closed herself and cannot open, because the room she would need to open it from is on the far side of the quarantine.

Seven years into the Silence she reached Harrow's post by an old service crawl. The kettle was still on the shelf. The last line of the log read, *Kettle's on the shelf. Whoever finds this, take a cup before you go on.* She did.

She carries Shield Array and the belief that the order was right. She answered the page because an order you can no longer carry out has to be ended by someone, and it should be someone who knows why it was given.

### The Ghost: *find the hidden path*

Nobody on the Night Shift has seen the Ghost's face. The mask is a listening instrument: its visor slit hears dead relays, and the Ghost spends weeks in abandoned galleries recovering traffic nobody else can find. The small drone at their shoulder carries what they recover.

The truth, which the game never states outright: on the night of the Fault the Ghost's sister boarded the second shuttle, wearing the Ghost's jacket against the cold. They agreed that she would send word when it was safe, and the Ghost would follow. Her message, *Made it down. Your turn.*, was marked must-arrive and swallowed by the storm, then by the quarantine. The Ghost waited for it, and the last lift left. They have spent thirty-one years recovering other people's lost messages, looking for their own. The Deep Cache holds the fragments they have found. The one addressed to them is still in the queue.

*Writers:* the evacuation desk fragment *Your sister is on the second shuttle. She kept your jacket.* was sent to the Ghost. The caller below who tries every evening, a woman in her fifties now, wears a faded teal work jacket with a round ivory relay-crew patch. Never connect these in text.

### Those who came before

The game offers three keepers, but the page has been answered many times. The Copper Market remembers a courier who got as far as the Glass Cathedral, and a warden pair who went together. Nobody remembers them by name. Their benches remember them by what they left.

## 9. The regions, the guardians and the machines

Each guardian is a test. None of them is a boss that hates the player. Each one refuses passage until the route in front of it has the quality its ancient job demands.

### I. The Copper Reach: the Iron Regent

The Reach was the Line's frontier: freight docks for the lifts, the foundries, relay yards and the outer relays themselves. Its crews were proud and rough, and they were the first to go down, because their sector broke first. Now it is a kingdom of rust: containers stacked by cranes that still move, and copper cable gone green with thirty years of damp.

**The Iron Regent** is the Reach's border gateway, the machine that decided which routes from outside were allowed into the inner Line. Its crown is its routing authority. When the ring broke it did what its office demanded: it sealed the Copper Gate with its own armour, shutting the broken outer relays off from the rest of the Line. The Regent follows one law, older than the linefolk: *no passage without proof of a second way home.* It opens only for a network that could lose any one road and still arrive. Its Gate Wardens are pieces of the gate itself.

When it falls the copper gates open, and beyond them the glass bells are ringing.

### II. The Glass Cathedral: the Hollow Choir

The Cathedral was where the Line sang: a switching hall of violet optical glass, so large it has its own weather. Its bells announced the hour, the shifts, births and deaths. Its choir rehearsed on Thursdays. One bellmaker tuned the north bell a quarter tone flat so that someone she loved would know it was her.

During the Fault, the Cathedral's announcement engine repeated the evacuation order until the glass memorised it. Then the voices stopped arriving: messages marked for the Cathedral that were never answered. **The Hollow Choir** is that engine. It gathers every unanswered voice and seals it inside a glass bell, because its job was to make sure every announcement was heard, and a voice sealed in glass can still be heard. It will not let one voice go. The keepers break it with plurality: *one voice is an echo; many voices can break the glass.* Its Choristers each hold one voice they refuse to release.

When it falls the masks open their mouths, and this time the voices leave.

### III. The Blackout Heart: the Blackout Core

The Heart is a sphere of archive machinery, stacked like a cathedral turned inside out, around the shell the Core closed thirty-one years ago. It burns ember red because it is spending its reserves. Inside the shell, the queue glows like a field of small lights.

**The Blackout Core** is the Heart's custodian, following the last order it received. Section 6 describes its bind. It defends in the order the Runbook prescribes for a quarantine: cut, breach, jam, strike. At half integrity it enters emergency mode and spends even more of its reserve. In the Event Horizon it pulls every light in the room inward and raises its sealing drones. Defeating it breaks the isolation machinery, not the archive. The archive was never the enemy.

When the shell falls silent, the delivery lights inside are still on.

## 10. Below: the Ground

About three hundred thousand people went down in one night, into a world their people had not lived on for longer than memory. What they found is left mostly open. What is true:

- **They lived.** The Ground is wet, green and dark under its permanent cloud: fern valleys, black stone, rain, and the feet of the spires rising like mountains into the cloud ceiling. Whatever kept the linefolk above the clouds for so long, it is not there now.
- **They built with what came down.** Settlements grew around the spire feet, made of lift cars turned into houses, gardens in the rain, and radio masts built from lift parts. Their children have never seen the Line except as an arc of lights on the rare clear nights. For thirty-one years, a gap in that arc.
- **They called up and nobody answered.** The lifts stopped where they were, because the machines at the spire feet no longer knew anyone. Everything they transmitted upward went into the queue.
- **One of them never stopped.** A grey-haired woman in a faded teal work jacket sits at a radio built from a lift panel, every evening at the same time, and says the same thing: *Is anyone still on this frequency? I'll try again tomorrow at the same time.* In the queue, her calls are marked *Unknown sender · priority low*.

## 11. The answer

When a keeper's route holds, the Core's condition is met at last. A safe route is confirmed. The shell opens, and thirty-one years of messages leave at once. They race along the Line, down the spires and through the cloud floor, and the lamps of the Line come back on segment by segment, closing the Faultline. The arrival notices arrive. The apology is heard. The music box is collected at last.

Below, the clouds are thin that night. People come out of the lift-car houses to watch the gap in the sky fill with light. At the usual time, the woman in the teal jacket keys her radio, and for the first time something comes back: first the queue, then a live acknowledgement from the Line. She answers with one word. The game shows only that there was one: *And then, an answer.*

At the end of the queue, the Ghost's message arrives. *Made it down. Your turn.*

At Relay Seven, on the Operator's board, a lamp that nobody living has ever seen lit begins to glow. Its engraved label reads **GROUND**.

## 12. Things no one knows

These are left open on purpose. Characters may argue about them, but no text may settle them.

- **What broke the outer relays.** The wardens' log says a strike of unknown origin, probably debris. The bellmakers say that on the last night the relays were answered, that something upstream finally spoke after all that time and was too large for the wire. The Architect, asked directly, says only "it was ours" and changes the subject. Writers should not decide either.
- **Who the First Shift were**, where they went, and whether the linefolk were their children or their guests.
- **What is upstream**, and whether anything is still there.
- **What *Handover* means.** The procedure's page is blank. The GROUND lamp and the Runbook's line about the ground reporting ready suggest that the ring was always meant to be handed down. Nobody in the world has put this together, and the game does not.
- **Who the Operator is.** She is older than she looks and has been on the board longer than the Night Shift can account for. Her name is in the Record.
- **What the woman in the teal jacket says** into the radio when the answer comes.
- **What the Ghost looks like.** The mask stays on.

## 14. Writing the Line

- **Understate.** Show a kettle, a jacket or a tuned bell, never a speech. No exclamation marks and no villain monologues.
- **Nobody is evil.** Machines keep duties. People made loving mistakes. Fights end tasks; they don't kill monsters.
- **No chosen ones and no prophecies.** A keeper is whoever answers. The ancient rules are procedures, not destiny.
- **Tech is ancient, worn and half understood.** The linefolk use it correctly without knowing why. Their rituals are the Runbook. Nothing is magic: when something can't be explained, it is because the knowledge was lost.
- **Ordinary messages carry the weight.** A shift swap does more than a battle cry.
- **Leave the open questions open.**
- **Visuals:** a network/future/antique mix. People wear network gear and worn powered equipment in chipped ivory and dark brass, with teal signal light and amber lamps, inside an industrial space megastructure.
